The ecosystem · packages/auth

Sign-in you don't write yourself

People sign in by a mailed link or at Google, GitHub or any OpenID Connect provider, plus a code from an authenticator app once they turn that on. Sessions live in your app's database, and middleware hands the signed-in user to your routes.

The package is a plugin that does the parts easy to get wrong: tokens kept in plain text, links that work twice, redirects to another site, OAuth without its state or PKCE. Your app keeps three: the pages, the email the link goes out in, and admit!, which says who gets in and which of your users they are.

How signing in goes#

By a mailed link. Auth.link!(email, then) stores the link (its token's hash, the address, where it goes after, its expiry) and gives you the URL. A link works once, for 15 minutes (link_ms). Your page posts its token to /auth/link; without a link_page, the URL is /auth/link itself.

At a provider. Your button links to /auth/<provider>?then=…. The plugin keeps a state, a PKCE verifier and a nonce in a cookie for ten minutes and sends the browser to the provider. Back at /auth/<provider>/callback, the state must match this browser's. The code is traded for who signed in: an ID token (OpenID Connect), or GitHub's API.

Under --dev. POST /auth/dev with any email signs it in at once, with no mail. Without --dev the route answers 404. It is for trying the app and for its tests: never start a server others can reach with --dev.

Your own way. Auth.sign_in!(answer, user) starts a session for a user you chose yourself (a password you checked, a person picked from a list) and sets its cookie. An app may sign people in this way alone, with no link or provider routes.

The other ways end alike: admit! is asked, a session starts, and the browser goes on, with the session cookie, to where it was going. That is always a path on your site: a link or a provider can't send anyone to another site.

Who gets in#

admit! is given who is signing in, an Auth.Identity:

FieldIs
provider : Str"email" for a link or the dev sign-in, else the provider's name
subject : Strwho they are there: the address, or the provider's own id for them
email : Strtheir address, lower-cased; "" when the provider has none it says is verified
name : Strtheir name, or ""
user : [New, Known(Str)]the user this identity signed in as before, even if its address has changed since
then : Strwhere the sign-in goes after: an invite's page, say, which admit! may act on

It answers Ok(user), your id for their user, or Err(why), which the sign-in page shows. It runs inside the transaction that starts the session, so its writes commit with the session (a new user's row, a welcome email queued as a job) or with its refusal (a request to join a waiting list). A database error rolls it all back. Keep slow work, such as a request to another server, out of it.

Sessions and middleware#

A session is a random token in a cookie, and a database row that keeps only its SHA-256. It lasts 30 days (session_ms). Used in its second half, it is extended by as long again, so requests don't write to the database each time.

  • Auth.required lets a signed-in request through. A signed-out page goes to your sign-in page and comes back after (?then=); anything else gets a 401 with the plugin's refusal.
  • Auth.required_with also reads your user's row: a session whose user is gone ends and answers as signed out. The example's Middlewares.roc uses it.
  • Auth.optional hands on SignedIn(session) or SignedOut.
  • Auth.sessions! lists a user's sessions, Auth.revoke! ends one, and Auth.sign_out_everywhere! ends them all.
  • A form that posts to /auth/sign-out signs out.
  • Your own way in: Auth.sign_in! (above), Auth.switch!(request, answer, user) when someone picks another person, and Auth.path!(then) for where they may go after.

Impersonation#

An admin helping someone can see the app as they do. Auth.impersonate!(request, user, ms) makes the admin's session act as that user for a while: every route sees them.

  • The session stays the admin's (session.real_user). It goes back when its time runs out or on Auth.stop_impersonating!, and each start and stop is logged with both users.
  • You decide who may impersonate before calling it; the example allows the addresses in ADMIN_EMAILS.
  • Use real_user to record who did what, and to guard what only a person may change themselves.
  • No two-factor code is asked of an admin acting as someone: nobody signs in.

Roles#

What a user holds, and why: Pro because they pay, because they are on a trial, or because an admin gave it. A role is one of your own tags, and so is the reason, so a misspelt one doesn't compile. Roles is a module of the package (import auth.Roles); its table comes with the plugin.

  • Someone holds a role while any of their grants of it lasts, one grant for each reason: a subscription that ends never takes away a gift, and a gift isn't cut short by a payment.
  • Granting again while a grant lasts moves its end, so a payment that grants until the next period's end each month stays one grant. Two grants at once give one.
  • Nothing is deleted but by forget!: grants that ran out or were revoked stay, so of! can show what ended when and who ended it, and you can tell someone had a trial already (the example's /trial does).
  • You decide who may grant before calling it, as with impersonation; each grant and revoke is logged. has! says no when the database can't be read, so what it guards stays shut.
  • Paid roles come from your payments: with Stripe, a function that reads the user's subscription and grants Pro until its period's end, or revokes it once it ended, run for every event about them. It reads the state, not the event, so events that come twice or out of order change nothing.

Two-factor sign-in#

With two_factor: Bool.True in the plugin's options, users may turn on two-factor sign-in. Then, after their link or provider, they enter the six-digit code their authenticator app shows (1Password, Google Authenticator, a phone's passwords app), or a recovery code. The codes are TOTP (RFC 6238), which every authenticator app makes.

Turning it on. Auth.two_factor_start!(user, account) makes a secret and gives you its otpauth:// URI, to show as a QR code (packages/qr's QrCode), and the secret as text for an app that can't scan. The first code the app shows turns it on and gives ten recovery codes, each good once in place of a code. Show them once: only their hashes are kept. The example's endpoints:

Auth.two_factor_disable! turns it off and Auth.two_factor_regenerate! makes new recovery codes, each given a code the app shows now, or a recovery code.

Signing in. For a user with it on, the link or provider doesn't sign in yet: the sign-in waits ten minutes for their code, and the browser goes to your code page (code_page, /sign-in/code). Meanwhile your middleware treats them as signed out, but sends a page to the code page, not the sign-in page. The code page posts the code to /auth/code:

  • A code is good now or for 30 seconds either side (a phone's clock may be a little out), and works once: a used code is refused, even in time.
  • A recovery code works once, and its use is logged.
  • A right code starts the session under a new token, so the token the browser waited with signs no one in. A wrong one comes back with ?error=code; the fifth ends the sign-in.
  • A user's wrong codes also count across sign-ins, turning it off and making new recovery codes: after ten in a row, their codes are refused for 15 minutes (TooManyTries, ?error=locked).

Who must have it is yours to decide: Auth.two_factor!(user) says whether it is on, and a route that requires it refuses those without. The example's admins must have it:

The secret at rest is sealed with the platform's AesGcm, under a key derived from APP_SECRET (32 characters or more: openssl rand -hex 32). With two-factor on, the server won't start without it, except under --dev, where a development secret stands in. Don't change it: every authenticator's codes would stop working, and only recovery codes would sign in.

The app rocx new makes has all of it: an account-page section that sets it up with a QR code and turns it on and off, and a code page.

Providers#

Register an app with the provider using the redirect URI below, after the server's public URL (PUBLIC_URL), and give the server its client id and secret. A provider without a client id isn't offered: Auth.providers! leaves its button out.

ProviderNeeds
Auth.google GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID, GOOGLE_CLIENT_SECRET; redirect URI /auth/google/callback
Auth.github GITHUB_CLIENT_ID, GITHUB_CLIENT_SECRET; callback URL /auth/github/callback
Auth.oidc({ name, label, issuer, authorize_url, token_url, client_id_env, client_secret_env }) any OpenID Connect provider; redirect URI /auth/<name>/callback

A record update changes any part of one: { ..Auth.google, client_id_env: "ACME_GOOGLE_ID" }.

When it fails#

A failed sign-in goes to your sign-in page with ?error= and where it was going (&then=):

errorMeans
linkthe link has been used or has expired (an expired one still says where it was going)
refusedadmit! said no; &reason= is what it said, &email= the address it turned away
providerthe provider refused, couldn't be reached, or isn't set up; &reason= says which
stateback from a provider too late, or in another browser
unavailablethe database couldn't take it just then
attemptsthe fifth wrong code ended a sign-in waiting for its code
lockedthe user had given ten wrong codes in a row: their codes are refused for a quarter of an hour after the last
timeoutno sign-in was waiting for a code in this browser: ten minutes went by

A wrong code goes back to your code page with ?error=code.

auth.SignInError words them: SignInError.message(query) gives the sentence for a sign-in page's query, and SignInError.field(query, "then") reads a field. It names no platform, so it works in the browser too.

The tables#

The plugin's migrations make its tables, the same on SQLite and Postgres: auth_sessions, auth_links (links on their way), auth_identities (which of your users each identity signed in as), and for two-factor auth_two_factor (sealed secrets), auth_recovery_codes (their hashes) and auth_waiting (sign-ins waiting for a code), and auth_roles (every grant, past ones too). Your users stay in your own table; the package knows them only by the id admit! gives.

Safety#

  • Tokens are 32 random bytes, kept only as their SHA-256: a leaked table signs no one in.
  • A link is used and deleted in one statement, so two clicks can't both sign in.
  • Cookies are HttpOnly and SameSite=Lax, and on HTTPS Secure and __Host-, so no other site under your domain can set them.
  • OAuth's state is compared in constant time, PKCE binds the code to the browser that asked, and the nonce binds the ID token to it. The ID token's issuer, audience and expiry are checked, and an address counts only if the provider says it is verified.
  • Links in mail use the server's public URL, never a request's Host.
  • An authenticator's secret is sealed (AES-256-GCM) and never kept in clear; recovery codes are 80 random bits, kept as their SHA-256.

A form on another site can't post to yours: the platform refuses it (why). Put a rate limit on your sign-in endpoint, per address and per client (its directory's +middleware.roc), so nobody can mail an address a hundred links. The app rocx new makes has it.

Status#

Everything above is built and tested. Passwords come next: they need a slow hash (Argon2) that the server's host will compute. Passkeys come later: they need the host to verify a P-256 signature, which it can make but not yet check. Both are in the package's PROPOSALS.md.
