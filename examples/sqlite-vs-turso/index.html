sqlite-vs-turso

One app timing the same database work on SQLite and Turso: writes alone and in a transaction, reads by key and by index, sums, JSON, a recursive query, eight callers at once (on their own rows, on one row, reading beside writing); a sale written to four tables in one transaction, with and without its job queued in it; then a million orders fetched one and a hundred at a time, and joined to their customers. Each is timed in Roc, in the server, around its Db calls. The results are on this page, except the sale's four workloads, not measured yet.

SourceREADMEAll the examples

What it shows#

  • The same SQL on two engines: --database-url picks one
  • Turso's concurrent writes (MVCC): a transaction that loses to another runs again
  • A job queued in its sale's transaction: the outbox pattern
  • Eight actors as eight callers at once, timed in Roc (Time.now_nanos!)
  • A million rows, loaded by SQL from a count, fetched and joined

The results#

Each workload is timed in Roc, in the server, around the Db calls it makes (Time.now_nanos! ), so no HTTP is in the numbers; the app built with --opt=speed as it would ship. Each engine keeps one file for the whole of it, as an app's database lives, written and read for a long time: after a small warm-up, the benchmark 10 times in a row. The buttons pick the first run, the first three or all of them, and the switch beside them leaves the first out of three or ten (runs 2–3, or 2–10), a cold start that may weigh on the rest; a cell gives their average, and under it the fastest and the slowest. Higher is better; a bar is a rate against its row's fastest, in bold.

First everyday work on 20,000 items, each run deleting the last run's and writing them again: what one caller does, then what eight do at once (eight actors told together, timed from the first told to the last done), then a checkpoint and the reads again.

Where eight write one counter at once, Turso's transactions (MVCC) lose to each other and run again: the runs say how many it took them, where SQLite's take turns and run once each.

Why Turso's MVCC reads slower. A row it has written stays in its store of versions, in memory, apart from the database's pages, until a checkpoint writes it there (Turso checkpoints when its log reaches about 4 MB). Reading such a row is a lookup in that store and a check of which version the reader may see. A scan through the store costs several times a scan of the pages ( scan ); walking an index and reading each row it names costs far more, and that is how every engine answers the group by: it walks the category index, to save a sort, and reads each row for its price ( aggregate ). With the index ruled out the same group by is a scan and a sort ( aggregate-no-index ); after the checkpoint the rows are in the pages, and the gap narrows (the -checkpointed rows). It doesn't close: MVCC checks every row it reads against its store, even a row in the pages, as the million rows below show; and what earlier runs wrote, kept as versions, slows later ones. The recursive query is slow on both of Turso's modes: that is Turso's engine, not its MVCC.

A million rows#

1,000,000 orders of 10,000 customers, a hundred each, spread over the table, with an index on whose they are: loaded by SQL itself from a count (a recursive query, 100,000 rows to a statement, each its own transaction), then checkpointed, so the reads find them in the database's pages, as an app's older data is (Turso's MVCC has checkpointed as it loaded: its log fills many times over). Then orders fetched one at a time and a hundred at a time (by a range of keys, and by the index), joined to their customers, and summed: the ten customers who spent most, and a sum over every order. 10 runs in a row, each making its tables afresh and loading them again, on a file of its own apart from the everyday run's: on Turso's MVCC what that run wrote would slow these reads, though it never touched these tables.
